Objective

I built an end-to-end model that underwrites a single-family rental in Dallas–Fort Worth and answers one question honestly: at today’s prices and a 7% mortgage, does buying a rental actually earn a return? It computes investment-return math (cap rate, NOI, DSCR, cash-on-cash, levered and unlevered IRR, and equity multiple) over a full 10-year hold.

The design has two layers. First it screens the DFW ZIP universe on rental economics to find the most promising submarket. Then it underwrites that ZIP in depth with a 10-year pro forma, a 360-month amortization schedule, two sensitivity tables, and a cap-rate exit. One constraint applies throughout: no manufactured returns. If the honest answer is “pass,” that is the finding.

1 Data & ETL (Power Query)

Loaded Zillow’s national home-value (ZHVI) and rent (ZORI) files from CSV, filtered them to the Dallas–Fort Worth–Arlington metro, and unpivoted each wide monthly series into a long table (ZIP · City · County · Date · Value). ZIP codes are kept as text.

Ranks #1–#3 are low-cost South Dallas and Lancaster ZIPs posting 10–12% “yields,” the classic profile of distressed-area risk the data can’t see (condition, taxes, tenant and vacancy risk). 3 Underwriting engine

Named inputs drive a 360-month amortization schedule that pays down to $0, and a 10-year pro forma: gross rent → vacancy → effective gross income → five escalating expense lines (tax, insurance, management, maintenance, CapEx reserve) → NOI → debt service → cash flow. The exit applies a terminal cap rate to forward NOI, net of selling costs and the loan payoff.

What the model found

  • Negative leverage. The asset yields about 4.3% unlevered, but the mortgage costs 7%. Financing a 4.3-cap asset with 7% debt reduces the return: the levered IRR (1.1%) lands well below the unlevered IRR (5.0%).
  • DSCR below 1.0 for the entire hold. Coverage runs 0.71 in Year 1 and reaches only 0.93 by Year 10, so NOI never covers the mortgage. Lenders typically require 1.20 or more.
  • Negative carry, all ten years. Year-1 cash flow is –$3,718 and is still –$877 in Year 10. The investment depends entirely on appreciation at exit.
  • Texas property-tax drag. At the full 2.24% non-homestead rate, Year-1 property tax is $4,878. That is the largest operating expense (46% of OpEx) and equal to about 52% of NOI, and it is the main reason a 9.6% gross yield shrinks to a 4.3% cap rate.
  • Conservative exit, same answer. The 5.0% exit cap is 70bp above the 4.3% entry cap. Exiting at the entry cap lifts the levered IRR to only about 5.1%, still well short of an 8% hurdle.

Conclusion

Pass at 7%. 76110 is the strongest conventional value the screen surfaced in DFW, and it still doesn’t clear the bar. At today’s price and rate it delivers roughly a 1% IRR, below risk-free Treasuries, in exchange for leverage, tenant and vacancy risk, illiquidity, and a decade of out-of-pocket carry. Against an 8% levered hurdle, it becomes worth revisiting only if the price falls to about $185K (–15%) at a 7% mortgage, or to about $196K (–10%) if rates ease to 5–6%. The value of the exercise isn’t a buy recommendation; it is a disciplined, evidence-backed pass.

What this project demonstrates

  • End-to-end build. Raw public data → automated ETL → quantitative screen → full underwriting model → stress testing → a written recommendation, all in one workbook.
  • Return math. Cap rate, NOI, DSCR, cash-on-cash, levered and unlevered IRR, and equity multiple.
  • Judgment over mechanics. Spotting a yield trap in the screen, using a forward rent-growth assumption (3%) instead of the recent 6.2% spike, and reaching an honest “pass” rather than forcing a positive number.
